Chapter I

Official rules

Official rule. A deck for constructed play has to meet three limits, all set out in one rule of the Comprehensive Rules.[1]

  • At least 60 cards. There’s no upper limit on deck size.[1]
  • One or two inks. A deck can’t contain more than two ink types. A dual-ink card shows two ink symbols and counts as each of its inks, so it only fits a deck that uses both.[1]
  • At most 4 copies per full name. A card’s full name is its name and version together, so two cards with the same name but different versions count as different cards.[1]

Which sets are legal, and which cards are banned, depends on the format. See Rules changes for format legality and bans.

Chapter II

The official mulligan

Official rule. Shuffle, choose a first player, and draw seven. Once, you may put any number of cards on the bottom of your deck, draw that many, then shuffle.[1]

The rules call this altering your hand. The starting player decides first, then each other player in turn order. The cards go to the bottom without being revealed, and anyone who altered shuffles and lets an opponent cut or shuffle the deck.[1] You can alter once in every game, not only after the first.

Chapter III

How to read the advice

Chapters I and II are official rules. Chapters IV to X are community advice: useful, but opinions, and the meta changes.

Where guides disagree, these chapters give the range instead of picking one number. Treat the figures as starting points, and each one cites the guide it comes from under References.

Chapter IV

Inkable count

Community adviceNot an official rule. Opinions from fan guides; see the cited sources.

Most guides suggest about 10 to 15 uninkable cards in a 60-card deck, which leaves roughly 45 to 50 inkables. They disagree on where in that range to aim.

  • 11–13 uninkables (47–49 inkables). Frank Karsten’s guide,[2] and Geeky Domain’s inkwell guide, which keeps uninkables under about a quarter of the deck.[3]
  • 10–13 uninkables. Geeky Domain’s beginner guide, which suggests 10 for new builders.[4]
  • 13–15 uninkables (45–47 inkables). Two TCG Protectors guides, with 13 as a safe start.[5][6]
  • No more than 15–19 uninkables. The Theory of Lorcana, quoting a probability article.[7]

One guide says top players aim for only 40–50% of the deck to be inkable.[8] That’s far below every other source and may be an error, so treat it with caution.

Aggro is the exception. It rarely needs more than 3 or 4 ink, so some guides let it run 18–20 uninkables.[5][6] Whatever the count, Karsten advises spending uninkable slots only on cards that are good in every matchup.[2]

Spread uninkables across costs, so you don’t get stuck holding expensive ones you can neither play nor ink.[3] Karsten also warns against altering aggressively when more than about 25% of the deck is uninkable.[9] Even with 20 uninkables, an opening seven with no inkables at all is only about a 0.02% chance.[7]

For context, about 20% of Core Constructed cards (sets 9 to 14) are uninkable. Items are the type most often uninkable, at about 40%.[10]

Chapter V

Cost curve

Community adviceNot an official rule. Opinions from fan guides; see the cited sources.

Guides agree that the curve should peak at 2 and 3 cost and thin out above that.[2][4][6] The exact counts differ:

  • Karsten’s sample midrange curve: 8 one-drops, 8 two-drops, 12 three-drops, 8 four-drops, 8 five-drops and 4 six-drops, plus 12 actions or songs.[2]
  • Geeky Domain: 6–10 at 1 cost, 10–14 at 2, 10–14 at 3, 10–14 at 4 and 5 together, and 4–8 at 6 or more.[4]
  • TCG Protectors and Antsy Labs: 8–10 at 1 and 2 cost, 10–12 at 3 and 4, 8–10 at 5 and 6, and 2–4 at 7 or more.[5][8]
  • TCG Protectors’ first-deck guide: 8–12 at 1 and 2 cost, 12–16 at 3 and 4, 8–12 at 5 and 6, and 2–6 at 7 or more.[6]

The archetype moves the curve. Aggro sits lower, control reaches up to 8-ink plays, and Sapphire ramp shifts the curve too.[2] Antsy Labs puts 12–14 low-cost cards in aggro.[8] Geeky Domain gives aggro more characters (24–26) and control fewer (18–20), with 14–16 actions and songs.[4]

Chapter VI

Archetypes

Community adviceNot an official rule. Opinions from fan guides; see the cited sources.

There’s no fixed list of archetypes, and guides draw the lines differently.[2][11] These five come up most often.

  • Aggro. Races for lore with cheap, high-lore characters. TCG Protectors uses about 15 lore by turn 5 or 6 as a benchmark.[11] Aggro decks alter hard for a low curve.[12]
  • Midrange. Controls the board early, then pivots to a lore push.[11] Karsten’s sample curve in chapter V is a midrange curve.[2]
  • Control. Uses board wipes and ink generation, with Emerald and Sapphire as one example pairing.[11] It plays fewer characters and more actions and songs.[4]
  • Ramp. Generates extra ink. Karsten names Sapphire ramp as its own style,[2] while TCG Protectors treats ramp as part of control, through cards like Sail the Azurite Sea.[11]
  • Songs. A theme deck built around songs.[2] It needs to balance singers against songs.[12] One estimate gives a 20-song deck about an 88% chance of drawing a song by turn 3 with none in hand, against about 60% for a 10-song deck.[7]

Guides also name other themes, such as Princesses and Evasives,[2] and typal decks such as Toy and Super, which are new in 2026.[11]

Chapter VII

Card roles

Community adviceNot an official rule. Opinions from fan guides; see the cited sources.

Guides sort cards by the job they do in a deck. One beginner guide names five roles: questers, contesters, interaction, engines and finishers.[4] A balanced deck has some of each.

  • Lore-getters. “Questers”: the characters that quest for lore.[4] Karsten counts Evasive characters among your win conditions and advises protecting them.[9]
  • Removal. Cards that banish or answer your opponent’s characters. Karsten suggests at least 4 removal cards,[2] and Antsy Labs suggests 4–8, plus 2–3 flexible “tech” slots for the current meta.[8] Removal was among the best-performing card types in the starter decks.[9]
  • Draw. Cards that draw more cards. Karsten suggests at least 4 card-advantage cards.[2] Geeky Domain groups draw and ramp together as “engines”.[4]
  • Bodyguard blockers. Characters that contest the board, Bodyguard being the usual example.[4] Karsten advises protecting them too, because they guard your win conditions.[9]
  • Finishers. Expensive cards that close out a game. Because they cost a lot, they usually need only 2–3 copies.[4]
Chapter VIII

Copy counts

Community adviceNot an official rule. Opinions from fan guides; see the cited sources.

The rules allow up to 4 copies of a card (chapter I). Guides favor 4 copies of your key cards over lots of single copies.[2][6] Karsten’s scale for the rest:[2]

  • 4 copies: essential cards you want every game.
  • 3 copies: strong cards where extra copies have diminishing returns.
  • 2 copies: slots that fill out the curve, or a 2–2 split between two similar cards.
  • 1 copy: cards you want available but don’t want to draw naturally.

Karsten’s “Rule of Eight” shows why counts matter. With 4 copies, you see at least one in your opening seven about 40% of the time, or 67% if you alter your whole hand. With eight cards that do a similar job, that rises to 65%, or 90% with a full alter.[2] Expensive finishers are the usual exception, at 2–3 copies.[4]

Upgrading a starter deck? One guide suggests swapping out the 4–8 weakest cards for 4-ofs.[6]

Chapter IX

Mulligan advice

Community adviceNot an official rule. Opinions from fan guides; see the cited sources.

Chapter II covers the official mulligan. Altering costs you no cards, so guides advise using it to get early plays and enough ink.[9][12]

  • Count your inkables. Two or fewer is a danger zone, 3–4 is healthy, and 5 or more is ink-rich.[12]
  • Ask two questions. Can you ink every turn, and do you have a meaningful play by turn 3? If not, alter. Look for one or two plays that cost 1–3.[12]
  • Build toward a curve. Keep the best card at each cost, bottom duplicates, and bottom some uninkables if you hold more than two.[9] Alter away hands full of expensive uninkables.[3]
  • Play or draw. On the play, favor proactive hands. On the draw, you can keep a slightly greedier hand.[12]
  • Archetype. Aggro should alter hard for a low curve, and song decks should keep a balance of singers and songs.[12]

How hard to alter depends on the deck. Karsten warns against aggressive alters when more than about 25% of the deck is uninkable.[9]

Chapter X

Worked example

Community adviceNot an official rule. Opinions from fan guides; see the cited sources.

Here’s one way to spread 60 cards across ink costs, following the guides in chapter V: a peak at 2 and 3 cost that thins out above that.[2][4][6]

The 24 cards at 2 and 3 cost give you something to play in the early turns. By the odds calculator, with 8 one-drops you hold at least one in your opening seven 65.4% of the time. With 12 three-drops, at least one is in hand by turn 3 on the play (9 cards seen) 88.7% of the time.

The 4 cards at 7 or more sit inside the 2–4 that two guides suggest for the top of the curve.[5][8] Try your own counts on the odds calculator.
